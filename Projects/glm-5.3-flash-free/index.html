The Festival

Five Nights of Light

Tihar is not one festival — it is five small thank-yous, one per evening, each with its own guest of honour: the crow, the dog, the cow, the goddess, and finally your own brother or sister.

It falls in the darkest fortnight of the year, on purpose. Exactly when the night is deepest, Nepal answers with lamps.

Day 1 · Kaag Tihar

Feed the Crow First

On the first morning, before anyone eats, a plate of rice is set out on the roof — and a crow takes the first bite.

In the old belief the crow is Yama's messenger, the postman between this world and the next. Feed him, the family says, and grief keeps its news one more day.

Day 2 · Kukur Tihar

The Guardian Gets His Day

The second evening belongs to the dog — the one who guards the gate, asks for no wage, and is cursed as often as thanked.

Once a year it reverses: every dog gets a marigold garland on his neck, a tika on his forehead, and the best plate in the house. He was watching all along. Now it is our turn.

Day 3 · Gai Tihar

The One Who Gives Daily

Then, the cow — milk for the child before dawn, dung that dries into winter fuel, the ox that pulls the field.

On the third day she is garlanded and her horns are painted. The logic of Tihar is right there: what gives to you without asking back is worthy of worship.

Day 3 · Night of Laxmi

Laxmi Checks the Doorways

That night, the goddess of fortune travels in the dark — and the story says she enters only the houses she can find.

So daughters of the house paint mandalas on the floor, footprints of welcome lead from the gate to the till, and a diyo is set into every corner that might stay dark. Where there is light, there is room for her.

Your Turn · Light the Way

Five Diyos, Waiting

Tap each clay lamp. When the last one catches, the night tilts toward you.

All five burn. Somewhere, a doorway just became findable. 🪔

Day 5 · Bhai Tika

Seven Colours Against the Dark

Five days end in the oldest ritual of all. A sister sits her brother down, circles him three times with oil and flowers, and paints seven colours on his forehead.

The old story says Yama, the god of death himself, was kept waiting by a sister's devotion — her tika out-argued him. So the brothers bring gifts, the sisters give years, and nobody leaves the table hungry.

“Long life, good luck — this year death has to get past your sister first.”
